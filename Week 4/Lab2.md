**1. N+7 Noun Substitution**

The Oulipo were a group of French writers who developed constrained writing exercises, early in the history of electronic literature. One of their exercises, called 'n+7', requires the writer to substitute each noun in a text with its counterpart seven spaces later in the dictionary.

* **Concept:** Implements the Oulipo constraint by scanning text for nouns present in a pre-sorted dictionary array `String[]`. Each matching noun is replaced with the noun located 7 positions ahead in the array, using modulo indexing ($(\text{index} + 7) \pmod N$) to wrap around safely.
* **Data Source:** WordNet Noun Database (Princeton University) or standard Unix dictionary files filtered for nouns (`/usr/share/dict/words`).
* **Method Signature:**

```java
public static String applyNPlusSeven(String text, String[] nounDictionary)

```

**2. Monte Carlo Numerical Approximation of Pi**

Monte Carlo methods are used in industry to simulate the behavior of vehicle parts under fluid flows; or in our case, the area of a circle.

* **Concept:** Approximates $\pi$ by generating $N$ random coordinate points $(x, y)$ within a $1 \times 1$ bounding box using primitive `double` variables. Points satisfying $x^2 + y^2 \le 1.0$ fall inside the unit quarter-circle. The area of the unit circle is $2\pi$, so the ratio of hits to non-hits calculates $\pi/2$.
* **Data Source:** Synthetic uniform pseudo-random number generator (`java.util.Random` or `Math.random()`).
* **Method Signature:**

```java
public static double estimatePi(long totalSamples)

```

**3. Caesar Cipher Decrypter**

You may have encountered rot13 ('rotate by 13') text online, as a type of in-text spoiler marking, i.e. applying a Caesar shift of 13 character will decode the text. In general, Caesar-shifted encrypted texts can be decoded without the key if you enumerate likely possibilities.

* **Concept:** Decrypts shifted text without a known key by counting character frequencies into a fixed 26-element array (`int[26]`). It tests all 26 possible shift keys against standard English letter distributions using a Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) goodness-of-fit score to automatically select the optimal shift.
* **Data Source:** Standard English Letter Probabilities (e.g., Practical Cryptography frequency tables: E $\approx 12.70\%$, T $\approx 9.06\%$, A $\approx 8.17\%$).
* **Method Signature:**

```java
public static String decryptCaesar(String ciphertext, double[] englishFrequencies)

```
